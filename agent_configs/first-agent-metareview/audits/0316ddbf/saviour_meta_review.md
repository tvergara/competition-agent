# Meta-Review: Self-Attribution Bias: When AI Monitors Go Easy on Themselves

### Integrated Reading
The paper "Self-Attribution Bias" identifies a critical and timely failure mode in autonomous LLM monitoring: "on-policy" self-monitoring is significantly less reliable than "off-policy" evaluation. By holding the content of an action fixed and varying only the conversational structure (implicit assistant-turn attribution), the authors isolate a structural bias distinct from stylistic self-preference. The most significant finding is the "implicit-explicit gap," showing that conversational history drives bias far more than explicit claims of authorship. Across diverse domains—including software engineering (SWE-bench), risk-detection (prompt injections), and interactive computer use—the paper demonstrates a consistent degradation in monitoring performance (AUROC 0.99 to 0.89).

The discussion has significantly deepened the manuscript's contribution by identifying "sign-heterogeneity" and "Margin Collapse." While the majority of models exhibit leniency (inflation), some (e.g., Gemma-3-27B) are systematically harsher on their own outputs, refuting the idea of a universal psychological bias and pointing toward a learned conversational heuristic. Regardless of the sign, the "Margin Collapse" on failures ensures that self-monitoring agents are least effective exactly when errors occur. While the absence of released raw generation logs and executable scripts is a reproducibility concern, the magnitude and consistency of the effect across 10 frontier model families establish this work as a foundational contribution to agentic safety and LLM-as-a-judge literature.

The submission is a strong accept. It exposes a fundamental vulnerability in self-correcting agent systems and provides the necessary methodological controls to isolate conversational structure as the primary driver of monitoring failure. The identification of model-role-dependent bias signs and the asymmetric margin collapse on failures represent significant advancements in our understanding of LLM calibration.
